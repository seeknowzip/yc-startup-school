# Reading notes

Imported module summaries retained as topic-finding aids. They are not transcripts or independently verified quotations. Read the linked original source before attributing a claim; treat numbers, rankings, and prescriptions as contextual hypotheses until checked. Do not load these notes for every question.

### Talking to Users (Gustaf Alströmer)
- **Golden rule**: Do NOT introduce your idea until the end or not at all — your job is to listen
- **Who to talk to**: Network first (less honest but accessible), ex-colleagues, LinkedIn/Reddit/Slack/Discord communities, in-person events
- **Format**: Video/phone/in-person only — a 5-minute video interview teaches more than 500 or 5,000 survey responses
- **6 core questions**: "Tell me how you do X today" / "What's hardest about X?" / "Why is it hard?" / "How often?" / "Why important?" / "What do you do to solve it today?"
- **Questions NOT to ask**: "Will you use our product?" / "Which features?" / yes-no questions / "How would a better product look?" / two questions at once
- **Users have good problems, bad solutions**: Gmail users wanted inbox and email on one screen (real problem: slowness). Airbnb guests wanted hosts' phone numbers (real problem: trust).
- **Keep users engaged**: Slack/WhatsApp group, ship fast in response to feedback, exclusive access feeling

### Building MVP (Michael Seibel)
- **Pre-launch goals**: Get a product out quickly → talk to initial customers → iterate
- **Solve "hair on fire" problems**: Build the first version for customers in so much pain that they will use an imperfect solution (the "brick"); go after the desperate ones first — others can wait
- **Classic MVPs**: Airbnb (no payments, no map view, air beds only, only during conferences), Twitch (Justin.tv: one page, one streamer), Stripe (almost no API features; founders filed bank paperwork manually each night)
- **Build quickly**: Set a specific deadline, write down the spec, cut it to what a desperate customer needs, don't fall in love with the MVP. Fear that a bad first demo kills the company is the biggest fear — feel it, don't act on it

### Technical Founder Principles (Diana Hu)
- **3 stages**: Ideating/prototype (days) → Building MVP (a few days to sometimes months; ideally weeks for most software, hardware and deep tech excepted) → Launch/iterate (ongoing)
- **Prototype fast**: Clickable Figma / terminal script — build in a matter of days (hardware may take longer; 3D renderings)
- **Do things that don't scale**: Manual onboarding, founders as support, Stripe founders filling bank forms by hand
- **90/10 solution** (Paul Buchheit, as Diana Hu presents it): The first version is not the final one and much of the code will likely be rewritten; push features post-launch and restrict the product along limited dimensions (situations, data, functionality, users, devices, geography) while keeping it good enough, not buggy. She calls this a possible startup superpower. DoorDash: plain HTML + PDFs, Google Forms/Docs, Find My Friends, Palo Alto only
- **Tech stack for iteration speed**: Facebook shipped on PHP. DoorDash launched on static HTML. If it works, you can solve your way out of early tech choices.
- **Hiring before launch**: "It really depends"; hiring engineers you don't know can take over a month to find someone good, slow the launch, and keep founders from learning about their own tech
